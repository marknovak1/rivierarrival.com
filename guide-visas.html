Settling in

Visas & paperwork

Everything I wish someone had told me before my first appointment at the Nice prefecture.

Nobody moves to France for the paperwork, and yet paperwork is where the first few months quietly go. I spent a whole Tuesday morning in a queue outside the prefecture in Nice before I understood that none of this is actually mysterious — it's just unfamiliar, and it goes in a specific order that nobody explains to you in advance. So here it is, in the order I eventually learned it.

Titre de séjour or long-stay visa — which is which

If you're not an EU citizen, the document that gets you into the country is usually a long-stay visa marked VLS-TS ("visa de long séjour valant titre de séjour") — it functions as your residence permit for your first year, so you don't walk out of the consulate with a separate card. What confused me at first is that this visa is not the end of the process; it's the beginning. Around three months before it expires, you apply to renew it as an actual titre de séjour — a physical card — at your local prefecture. EU citizens have it easier — no titre de séjour and no formal registration requirement at all, though prefectures can still ask for proof of address or income for certain administrative tasks, so it's worth keeping those handy since it comes up more often than the rules suggest it should.

Validating it with OFII

Before any of the prefecture business starts, your VLS-TS visa needs to be validated online with the OFII (Office Français de l'Immigration et de l'Intégration) — validation itself is done through an online form, but a medical visit is still a genuine requirement for many people, depending on nationality and visa type, rather than an occasional exception. You do this within around three months of arriving, and there's a small tax stamp fee involved, paid online — the amount depends on your category (students pay less, around €50; other categories vary), so it's worth checking what applies to your own visa type rather than assuming a flat figure. Miss the window and you'll spend a frustrating amount of time explaining yourself later, so I'd put it on your calendar the week you land, not the week you remember you should.

Getting a prefecture appointment

Many renewal appointments in the Alpes-Maritimes run through the ANEF portal (Administration Numérique des Étrangers en France), though it depends on your category — some are still handled in person or by email rather than online. You create a dossier online, upload your documents, and wait — and the waiting is real; slots for the Nice prefecture can book out weeks or months ahead depending on the season, so I always tell people to start the renewal request the moment they're eligible, not when the current card is about to expire. If your dossier is accepted, you'll usually get a récépissé — a paper proving you're mid-renewal — which legally covers you while the actual card is being produced. Keep it with your passport; you'll be asked for it more than once.

What to bring

Passport, your current visa or titre de séjour, proof of address (a recent electricity bill or a signed lease works — an Airbnb booking generally doesn't), passport photos taken to the specific French format, and financial proof if your status requires it. Any foreign document that isn't already in French — birth certificates are the classic one — needs a certified translation from a traducteur assermenté, and some documents need an apostille from the country that issued them — though not always: EU documents, and documents from countries with a bilateral agreement with France, often don't need one, so it's worth checking whether yours actually does before you arrange it. Bring three photocopies of everything. Not two. I learned this the annoying way, standing at a desk while an agent photocopied my own passport back to me and charged me for the privilege.

Common mistakes

The big ones I see over and over: missing the OFII validation window because nobody mentioned there was one; assuming a hotel or short-term rental counts as proof of address; turning up with a translation that isn't from a certified translator and being turned away on the spot; and waiting until the last possible month to start a renewal, which turns a routine process into a stressful one. None of these are hard to avoid once you know they're coming — that's really the whole point of this page.

How long it actually takes

Realistically: OFII validation, a few weeks once submitted. A prefecture appointment slot, anywhere from a few weeks to a few months out, depending on demand. The card itself, once your dossier is approved, often takes several more weeks to be produced and arrive — which is exactly why the récépissé exists, so you're not left in limbo. All told, budget three to six months of this running in the background of your first year, and you'll be pleasantly surprised more often than not.

A quiet aside: the person who made this whole process bearable for me wasn't an official, it was another expat at the café next to the prefecture who'd done it the year before and talked me through what to expect while we both waited for our numbers to be called. If you're going through this now, you're not the only one — and it does end.

This reflects my own experience and general practice as of writing — requirements, fees and portals do change, so it's always worth double-checking the current details on the official prefecture or France-Visas site before your appointment.
