sections:
  - heading: "Do it in an order"
    body: |-
      The people who arrive calm are not the ones who found the prettiest apartment on a Sunday night. They are the ones who did the move in an order. The long-stay visa is requested before you pack the house. A real address comes before the bank, the doctor and the school forms. The town comes before the furniture.

      I moved here from abroad myself. The first year was a lot of queues and a lot of French I did not yet have. This page is the map I use now when someone writes from the US or Canada and says they want to be here for good, or at least for longer than a season.

  - heading: "A visit is not a move"
    body: |-
      If you hold a US or Canadian passport, you can come for a short stay without a short-stay visa. France's own embassies in the United States and Canada describe that exemption: 90 days in any 180-day period in the Schengen area, under the European list of visa-exempt nationalities.

      There is a second, older arrangement on top of that, and it is easy to get wrong. A bilateral agreement lets US citizens (since 1949) and Canadian citizens (since 1950) add another 90 days in metropolitan France, but only after the Schengen 90 days, and only if you say so when you cross the French border so the officer can adjust the entry record. You cannot spend the bilateral days first and then hop to Italy as if the clock had reset. The French embassy pages explain the sequence. It is a way to lengthen a scouting trip. It is not permission to live here.

      On a short visit, border police can still ask for a passport, proof of where you are sleeping, insurance that covers medical care, hospital costs, repatriation and death, and enough money for the days you are staying. France-Visas publishes the daily amounts used at the French border: 65 euros a day if you have a hotel booking, 120 euros a day if you do not, and a mix of the two if the booking only covers part of the trip. Those figures are for a short stay. They are not the test for a residence card.

      If the plan is to stay past what those short-stay rules allow, you need a long-stay visa before you come, unless your nationality is one of the few excused from it. US and Canadian citizens are not on that short list. The interior ministry's visa-exemption page keeps long-stay exemptions for EU, EEA and Swiss citizens, and for Monaco, Andorra and San Marino.

  - heading: "Ask for the long-stay visa before you leave"
    body: |-
      France-Visas is plain about this. A stay of more than 90 days needs a long-stay visa in advance, if your nationality does not excuse you. The visa itself lasts somewhere between three months and a year. Staying past that means a residence permit from the prefecture.

      There are two shapes. A long-stay visa that counts as a residence permit (you will see it called a VLS-TS) covers a stay of up to a year and means you do not apply for a separate card at the start. You do still have to register it. A long-stay visa marked "carte de séjour à solliciter" means you must ask the prefecture for the actual card after you arrive. Some work routes, including the ones France-Visas groups under "passeport talent", can lead to a multi-year card. Which file is yours depends on why you are coming: a private stay, work, study, or family. The visa assistant on France-Visas is the checklist. I can tell you which conversation to have. I can't lodge the application for the consulate.

      If you will not be working, the private-stay route is the one retirees usually use. I wrote that up separately, with the resource floor Service-Public publishes, on [Retiring on the French Riviera](guide-retiring-on-the-french-riviera.html). If you are coming for a job, a course, or to join family, say so at the start. Those files are different, and a visitor file that forbids work will not quietly turn into a work permit after you land.

  - heading: "The first errand after you land"
    body: |-
      If your visa is a VLS-TS, France-Visas says you validate it within three months of arrival, online, on the étrangers en France portal. You need the details printed on the visa, the date you entered France, your address here, an email address, and a card to pay the fee the portal charges for issuing the residence permit. Miss the three months and France-Visas is blunt: you are no longer in regular stay, and you cannot cross a Schengen border again until that is fixed.

      If the visa instead says you must request a residence card, you do that at the prefecture of where you live, within two months of arrival, and you do not start that particular step with OFII. Around Nice, that prefecture is Alpes-Maritimes. The longer, lived version of the prefecture, the récépissé and the pile of photocopies is in [Visas & paperwork](guide-visas.html).

  - heading: "A home, then everything that needs an address"

      Which town is a real decision, not a mood. Nice if you want a city and an airport. Antibes if you want a harbour and a school run. Menton if you want quiet. I keep the sketches on [Neighborhoods](neighborhoods.html).

  - heading: "Children, retirement, and the first month"
    body: |-
      Families ask me about schools before they ask about neighbourhoods, which is the right instinct and the wrong order only if the school is an hour from the apartment. The schools I am willing to name, because I have read them on their own sites, are on [International schools in Nice and along the Riviera](guide-international-schools.html).

      If the move is a retirement rather than a job, start with [Retiring on the French Riviera](guide-retiring-on-the-french-riviera.html). The visitor card has a published income floor, a health-insurance condition, and a ban on working. Those are not details to discover at the consulate window.

faqs:
  - question: "Do US or Canadian citizens need a visa to move to Nice?"
    answer: |-
      For a short visit, no short-stay visa. France's embassies describe a Schengen stay of 90 days in any 180, plus a bilateral extra 90 days in metropolitan France if you follow the order and declare it at the French border. To live here past that, you need a long-stay visa before you move. US and Canadian citizens are not excused from long-stay visas.
  - question: "Can I arrive without a visa and sort the residence permit after I land?"
    answer: |-
      The normal path is the other way around. France-Visas says a stay of more than 90 days needs the long-stay visa in advance. Once you have it, a VLS-TS is validated online within three months, and a visa that tells you to request a residence card means a prefecture application within two months. I would not plan a move that depends on switching status after a visa-free entry.
  - question: "Moving to Nice from the US, where do I start if I don't know the towns?"
    answer: |-
      Start with how you will spend a Tuesday, not with a photograph of the sea. Airport and city life point to Nice. A school run and a harbour point to Antibes and the towns around Sophia Antipolis. Quiet points to Menton. The town notes are on the neighborhoods page, and I am happy to talk it through before you book viewings.
