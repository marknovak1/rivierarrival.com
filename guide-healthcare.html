Settling in

Healthcare

The Carte Vitale, the mutuelle, and finding a doctor who'll actually see you.

French healthcare is genuinely good — that part isn't exaggerated. What takes getting used to is the two-layer system: a public reimbursement scheme (assurance maladie) that covers most of most things, and a private top-up (mutuelle) that covers the rest. Here's how it actually works in practice, not in theory.

How the system actually works

You pay for most appointments and prescriptions upfront, then get reimbursed — usually around 70% for a standard doctor's visit — by the state system, with the remaining portion picked up by your mutuelle if you have one. Once your Carte Vitale is active, a lot of this reimbursement happens automatically without you filing paperwork, which after the visa process feels almost suspiciously easy.

Getting your Carte Vitale

You apply through your local CPAM (Caisse Primaire d'Assurance Maladie) once you're eligible — generally after you've been in the system a few months and your residency status is settled. The application is mostly online now, with a form and supporting documents, and then comes the wait: realistically, budget several months for the physical card to arrive once your file is submitted, sometimes longer — it varies a lot by region and by how complete your paperwork was the first time round. Before it does, keep every prescription and receipt — you can usually still get reimbursed retroactively once the card lands, it just takes an extra step.

Choosing a médecin traitant

This is your declared primary doctor, and registering one matters more than it sounds — seeing a doctor without a declared médecin traitant on file usually means a lower reimbursement rate and sometimes a referral you didn't know you needed. Ask neighbors or colleagues who they see; a personal recommendation carries a lot of weight here, and it's normal to visit a doctor once just to see if it's a fit before formally declaring them.

The mutuelle top-up

A mutuelle is optional in theory and close to essential in practice, especially for dental, optical and hospital stays where the public reimbursement alone leaves a real gap. If you're employed, your employer is generally required to offer one and often covers part of the cost. If you're not, you'll want to compare a few independent options — prices and coverage levels vary a lot, and it's worth reading what's actually included rather than just the headline price, since a cheap plan with a low dental ceiling can end up costing more than a slightly pricier one the first time you actually need it.

I made the mistake early on of picking the cheapest mutuelle I could find without reading past the first page, and only found out what it didn't cover when I needed a filling. It wasn't a disaster, just an unnecessary out-of-pocket surprise — the kind of thing ten extra minutes of reading would have avoided entirely.

Finding an English-speaking doctor

Nice has a large enough international community that English-speaking doctors do exist, particularly for general practice and dentistry — it's just not always obvious from a name on a door. Word of mouth in expat groups is the fastest way to find one, and it's worth asking specifically rather than assuming; even a doctor whose English is limited to the exam room can be a perfectly good médecin traitant if the actual care is good. For anything more specialized, larger private clinics in Nice tend to have at least one practitioner comfortable working in English, even if the front desk isn't — it's always worth calling ahead and asking plainly.

Pharmacies, and what to do before your card arrives

French pharmacies (look for the green cross) are a genuinely useful first stop for minor ailments — pharmacists here can advise and often recommend over-the-counter treatment before you need to see a doctor at all. Before your Carte Vitale exists, you'll pay full price and keep the paper receipts (feuilles de soins); once your CPAM file is set up, submit them for reimbursement rather than throwing them away, even if it takes a few weeks to land.

Common mistakes

Waiting to apply for the Carte Vitale because the process feels intimidating — it isn't, it's just slow, so starting early matters. Skipping a mutuelle and then being surprised by a dental bill. And not registering a médecin traitant, which quietly costs you money on every visit until you do.

I went the better part of a year without a Carte Vitale — partly because I didn't realize I could apply as early as I did, partly because the wait itself just runs long. Don't do what I did on the first count — start the file the moment you're eligible, and let it run in the background while you get on with everything else.

Reimbursement rates, eligibility timing and CPAM processes shift from time to time — always double-check current details with your local CPAM or ameli.fr before relying on them.
